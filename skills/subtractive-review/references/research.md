# Research Basis

## Primary source

Gabrielle S. Adams, Benjamin A. Converse, Andrew H. Hales, and Leidy E. Klotz, "People systematically overlook subtractive changes," *Nature* 592, 258–261 (2021). <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03380-y>

Across a series of experiments, participants were asked to improve objects, patterns, writing, itineraries, and organizations. The same pattern held across tasks:

- **People default to adding.** When improving something, most participants proposed additions, even when removing an element was the simpler and better change.
- **The failure is in search, not in judgment.** When subtractive options were made salient, participants recognized and chose them. They had not rejected subtraction; they had not thought of it.
- **An explicit cue helps.** Telling participants that removing is allowed or free raised the share of subtractive solutions.
- **More chances help.** Participants who had more practice or more attempts were more likely to find the subtractive option. The first idea is the most additive.
- **Cognitive load hurts.** Participants under extra mental load were less likely to find subtractive solutions and fell back on additive ones.

Klotz expands the findings and their cultural and organizational causes in *Subtract: The Untapped Science of Less* (Flatiron Books, 2021).

## How this skill applies the findings

| Finding | Skill mechanism |
|---|---|
| Default additive search | Core rule: no additive change is accepted until a subtractive alternative is generated |
| Failure is in search, not judgment | Step 3 walks a fixed lens catalog so the search does not depend on recall |
| Explicit cue helps | Step 3 begins with a written cue that removal is allowed and free to propose |
| More chances help | Every lens is walked even after a good candidate is found |
| Cognitive load hurts | The lens catalog and output template carry the load so the reviewer does not have to |

## Limits

The studies measure how people generate solutions, not whether subtraction is always better. This skill keeps the goal from step 1 as the judge, uses Chesterton's fence before any removal, and accepts `add-justified` as a valid verdict.
